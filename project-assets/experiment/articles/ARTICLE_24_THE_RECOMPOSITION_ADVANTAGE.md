# The recomposition advantage: why independents should not try to out-scale the networks

The wrong conclusion from the agency AI shift is that everyone now needs to become a mini holding company.

That instinct is understandable. If the large networks are reorganizing around data, media, identity, commerce, creator systems and AI-enabled delivery, many independents will assume they need the same menu. More capabilities, more specialist boxes, more visible breadth. On the surface, that looks like adaptation.

In reality, for many independents, it is a trap.

Because the strategic opportunity is not to imitate the scale logic of the networks. It is to exploit the coordination weakness that scale creates.

That is the real opening.

If the last year has clarified anything, it is that the market is not rewarding agencies simply for being creative at volume. The big groups themselves are saying as much through their behavior. Publicis keeps strengthening its integrated model across data, media, identity, creator and commerce. WPP is responding to pressure by simplifying its structure and compressing its operating model. Omnicom continues to justify the IPG deal not as a creative expansion but as a way to increase precision, full-funnel capability and operating efficiency.

The signal underneath all of this is consistent. The premium is moving away from creative scale alone and toward the ability to coordinate more capabilities into a coherent commercial system.

But that does not mean every player should pursue the same answer.

For the large groups, reorganization is about reducing the drag of complexity at scale. For independents, the advantage should be the opposite: preserving enough sharpness, proximity and decision density that complexity never gets embedded in the first place.

That distinction matters because many independent agencies are about to make an expensive strategic mistake. They will respond to the market by adding service lines faster than they can truly integrate them. More media. More PR. More content. More AI consultancy. More commerce. More production layers. More specialist labels. The result will look modern in credentials decks and website navigation. Internally, it will often make the business slower.

Every new capability sounds like optionality. In practice, it can create handoffs.

And handoffs are where independent advantage goes to die.

The deeper issue is that most clients do not actually experience agencies as capability inventories. They experience them as decision systems. How quickly does this team understand the problem? How quickly can they move from ambiguity to direction? How many loops are required before something is live? How well do strategy, creation, distribution and execution arrive as one coordinated movement instead of a relay race?

That is where independents can beat the networks.

Not by offering more boxes, but by offering tighter recomposition.

Recomposition is different from integration in the usual agency sense. Integration is often treated as a governance promise, separate teams working together under a shared story. Recomposition is more demanding. It means the agency can continuously reassemble the right combination of strategic, creative, distributional and operational capabilities around the client problem without defaulting back into departmental logic.

That is a speed advantage, but also a clarity advantage.

It allows a smaller firm to compete not on breadth in the abstract, but on how quickly it can turn multiple disciplines into one market move.

This is where independents should focus. Not capability breadth, but recomposition speed.

That changes the target model.

Instead of organizing around departmental excellence with occasional collaboration, the stronger independent model may be the integrated client pod. A small cross-functional unit with enough strategic, creative, production and distribution intelligence to move without repeated escalations. Not a giant all-in-one team, but a tight structure that reduces translation loss.

Instead of bringing distribution in after the creative idea is approved, distribution needs to enter at the briefing stage. Not as a media add-on, but as a shaping force. If an idea only works once it reaches the market, then the route to market cannot be a downstream concern.

Instead of treating AI as a visible specialist offer that sits beside the business, independents should treat it as an internal speed and margin layer. Its job is not to create more presentation theater. Its job is to compress low-value production, increase iteration speed, strengthen synthesis and make smaller teams more dangerous.

And instead of evaluating work mainly through the lens of creative quality, independents should increasingly judge it through four linked questions: how fast did we decide, how fast did we go live, how well did distribution amplify the idea, and what behavioral effect did the work create?

That sounds operational, but it is strategic.

Because once production becomes easier to access, the scarce asset is not output. It is coordinated momentum.

This is why the independent opportunity is so interesting. Smaller firms do not need to win the capability arms race. They need to win the compression race. They need to show that fewer people, arranged better, can outperform larger organizations arranged worse.

That is a much more defensible position than pretending to be a smaller version of a network.

It also has a clearer economic logic. Building broad capability benches is expensive. It increases overhead, complicates positioning and often forces agencies to feed the machine with work that keeps specialists occupied rather than work that solves the client’s actual problem. A recomposition model is different. It rewards fluidity, trust, judgment and fast cross-functional assembly. It turns compactness into leverage.

The contrarian implication is that saying no becomes as strategic as saying yes. Independents may need fewer service lines than they think, provided the ones they keep are genuinely interoperable. The goal is not maximum breadth. The goal is minimum drag.

That may feel counterintuitive in a market where every agency wants to look complete. But completeness is overrated if it comes at the cost of speed, density and coherence.

Clients rarely remember the size of the menu. They remember whether the agency made the hard thing move.

So the real question for independents is not, what else should we add?

It is, what structure would let us turn strategy, creation, distribution and execution into one tighter movement than the networks can produce?

That is the better ambition.

And if the reorganization premium is the main prize of the AI era, then the independent version of that premium may not belong to the broadest agency.

It may belong to the one that recomposes fastest.
